\chapter{Data}

\section{Data structure}
This thesis will cover a rather large (at least in the machine learning sense) climate data set. The data set covers two time periods; the "historical" period, spanning from 1951 till 2015, and the "future" data set, spanning from 2015 till 2100. In the data we find "images" of the climate, each image contains a number of \glspl{channel}, which covers one climatological variable each (e.g. temperature, precipitation, or wind). One "image" spans one day, and contains an aggregate of data from that day (either minimum, mean, or maximum). The dataset will cover two different climate models, a so-called \gls{gcm} and an \gls{rcm}. Each model covers two carbon emission scenarios (\gls{126} and \gls{370}), in the "future" time period. Additionally, each model has 3 \glspl{realisation} (ensembles) over the entire time period.

A visualisation  of the data structure can be found in \autoref{fig:data_structure}.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering

\subsection{Climate models} \label{sec:data-climate-model}
The machine learning task in this thesis is, of course \gls{downscaling}, which means that data must exist in two different resolutions. In this case, the two resolutions are populated by two different climate models. The low-resolution data is created by the \gls{ece} \gls{gcm}, which operates at a 1° grid (roughly 75 km). The high-resolution data is created by the \gls{hclim} \gls{ddm}, and operates at a rotated pole grid with 0.11° resolution (12.3 km) over the EURO-Cordex domain. 

Naturally, the two climate models operate on two different grid projections. While the \gls{ece} data is on a regular latitude-longitude grid,the \gls{hclim} data uses a rotated polar grid. As the two projections map the Earth in wildly different manners, the \gls{ece} data set has to cover a much bigger area, than the \gls{hclim} data, to make sure that it encompasses all the of the \gls{hclim} area. The domains of the two datasets have been visualized in \autoref{fig:domains}.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/data_section/domains.png}
    \caption{Spatial areas of the two climate model datasets. The blue line represents the outer edge of the \gls{ece} (\gls{gcm}) data, and the orange-red line represents the outer edge of the \gls{hclim} (\gls{ddm}) data.}
    \label{fig:domains}
\end{figure}

In climate science, a climate model is a numerical estimator, which takes satellite data as input, as well as a set of hyperparameters, and uses an expensive physics-based method to calculate how the climate looks on three-dimensional grid (two spatial dimensions and one temporal). In other words, the models take the measured data and estimates how the corresponding climate look on the entire planet, while also estimating a number of years into the future. As the models are highly dependant on their initialisation it is customary to run several \glspl{realisation} to model the climate in a less biased fashion. To make sure, that such internal variation is accounted for, this data set includes 3 \glspl{realisation} for both the historical data, and both scenarios

\subsection{Scenarios} \label{sec:scenarios}
In the future period, for both data sets, there are two \glspl{scenario}; "\gls{126}" and "\gls{370}". SSP is short for "Shared Socioeconomic Pathways" and they describe different versions of how much emission is produced until the year 2100. \gls{126} is on the mild, green side of the spectrum, modelling a more sustainable future, where the excess energy on earth eventually declines as the human race start correcting their mistakes. \gls{370} is a more conservative estimate, in which the emission level will rise every year, rapidly heating up the planet.

\subsection{Static data}
Besides the two climate models, there are also two static features. In this sense "static" means that these features do not change over the temporal dimension. The two variables are "Orography", which describes the topography of the target area, and a "Land-Water mask" which describes the ratio of the grid point covered by water. Both features are delivered at a resolution matching the target grid.

\subsection{Variables}
As mentioned in \autoref{sec:scenarios} the \gls{ece} and \gls{hclim} data has 18 and 4 variables respectively. In this case there are two types of variables, single-level variables and pressure-level variables. Single-level variables are only recorded once and typically model surface level climate like 2 meter temperature or precipitation. Pressure-level variables are recorded on several levels of the atmosphere. In this data we are working with three different pressure levels 500, 700, and 850 hPa, and on each level we see variables like pressure, humidity, etc. See \autoref{tab:variables} for full list of variables.

\subsection{Regridding} \label{sec:regridding}

As it was mentioned in \autoref{sec:data-climate-model}, the two data sets for this thesis are defined on two different grids. They are not the same size, not the same resolution, and neither do they use the same projection of the planet.
However, it happens in certain parts of the project, that comparisons have to be made between these data sets, which require point by point computations. As such the \gls{ece} data will be "regridded", i.e. the data will be interpolated and cropped to fit the grid, size, and projection of the \gls{hclim} data. As it is costumed in climate science a bilinear interpolation will be used for temperatures while a conservative interpolation will be used for precipitation (as this is a mass). The regridded data will henceforth be referred to as "regridded" or "regridded \gls{ece}"

\section{Data splits}

 Now that the distribution of data has been analysed, it is possible to make some assumptions, which will help design data splits for the experiments. But before doing so, let's discus the purpose of the final model. It is important to design the test-split around what the model, in the end should compute. In this case, the purpose of the model is to downscale other \glspl{realisation} of the same \gls{gcm}-\gls{ddm} pair. Therefore, it is obvious to allocate entire \glspl{realisation} for testing. But how many, and which ones?

 In \autoref{sec:data-distribution} we argue that the first \gls{realisation} (r1i1p1f1) has the most centred distribution of data, in respect to "r2" and "r3". "r2" and "r3" are either on the lower or higher side. It is not know, whether "r1" is centred in the total population of \glspl{realisation}, however it cannot be an extremity. "r2" and "r3" on the other hand could potentially lie at the very edge of the distribution. As a rule of thumb, in machine learning, it is not ideal to train on extremities, as it will give the model an unrealistic idea of the total population. With these arguments it seems obvious to use \gls{realisation} 1 for training and \gls{realisation} 2 and 3 for testing.

 \section{Climatology}

The next step is to create a reference model for the climate change through the \gls{hclim} dataset. This is done to gain an understanding of how current downscaling methods perform, not on a specific level but on a more general climate level. Eventually this can be a means to quantify RQ\ref{it:rq3}. More specifically, we will be evaluating three different climate indecies (all from the Expert Team for Climate Change Detection and Indecies - \href{https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml}{ETCCDI}); annual summer days, annual frost days, and annual heavy precipitation days. The analysis will be carried out on three temporal periods; the historical 1985-2014 (used as reference), the mid century (2020-2050), and the late century (2070-2100). All of which have been shifted by 2 months to generalise the seasons of each year. 
Furthermore, to show regional changes, we will be using three of the IPCC reference regions v4 (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change - \href{https://interactive-atlas.ipcc.ch/regional-information}{IPCC}); Northern Europe (NEU), Central Europe (CEU), and the Mediterranean (MED). See figure \autoref{fig:ipcc_regions}.

\subsection{Annual summer days}

The annual summer days index is a standardised index to show the change in temperature over time. To detach from the absolute temperature values, and instead focus on general climate change, the metric measures the annual number of days in which the temperature exceeds 25°C. In our data set, since it only contains daily data, this is modelled as the maximum temperature (tasmax) exceeding 25°C. The model is made per grid point and will thus show a spatial map of the mean number of summer days over the chosen periods. The figure is shown in \autoref{fig:summer_days}.

To recap, it would be expected of this figure to show a small change in the mid century periods and a larger change in the two late periods as the globe heats up. Additionally, it would be expected to see a larger change in the \gls{370} \gls{scenario}, as this has a higher warming level. For the histograms in the bottom row it would be expected to see few summer days in the northern region (NEU), with slightly more in the central region (CEU). In the Mediterranean (MED) region it is expected to see more summer days than not. Looking at \autoref{fig:summer_days} we see that the figure is more or less as expected. Land areas have more summer days than oceanic ones, and the further south the more summer days. Northern Europe indeed has a lot fewer summer days than the Mediterranean. Not even climate change can break those rules. The top row of plots shows that Europe (and Northern Africa) ranges from 0 all the way to around 300 annual summer days. In \gls{126} and the mid century \gls{370} images there is a clear distinction between water regions and land regions, and the border of regular summer days lies just south of Denmark. In the late century \gls{370} \gls{scenario} however, the Mediterranean heats up, and produces around 200 annual summer days, even in areas far from the coast. Additionally the border of regular summer days rises to lie somewhere on latitude of Northern Skåne.

The second row confirms the image, just described. In general it is clear that the areas hid the hardest by the rise in temperature is the Mediterranean sea, with areas in the late century \gls{370} increasing upwards of 100 annual summer days. A similar story is seen in the Black Sea with the worst development in the Red Sea.

The bottom row shows us a regional view of the change in distributions of the annual summer days. As described we see a clear difference in the three regions. Furthermore, it shows a quantitative view of the increase in annual summer days from the historical reference period to the various warming scenarios. This further enhances the picture of how big the difference is from the historical period to the late century \gls{370}. Let's zoom in on the Northern European (NEU) region. Differently from what was clear in the two rows above, this plot shows the relative difference in summer days much more clearly. It shows that the maximum number of annual summer days will almost double. In the two other regions; Central Europe and the Mediterranean, the distribution is similarly scaled towards a higher number of annual summer days. 

In summary, the summer day index paints a picture of how and where the climate will change. Additionally, we see how the different temporal periods and SSP \glspl{scenario} have an effect. While the two mid century images have a smaller change, all warming \glspl{scenario} show an increase in annual summer days.

\subsection{Annual frost days}

While the annual summer days index shows how "warm" the climate will be through time, it is also important to look at how "cold" it will become. Could the temperature simply spread more, meaning there would be more of both warm and cold days? In \autoref{fig:frost_days} we see the distribution of the so-called "annual frost days index". Frost days are defined as days in which the minimum temperature (tasmin) subceeds 0°C. The index is important to show the change in icy and snowy regions. It answers the previous question clearly: The climate is getting warmer.

When looking at frost days, it is expected to see two features highlighted; mountains and ice sheets. In this domain there are no permanent ice sheets or sea ice, however, ice might drift from Greenland to the Denmark Strait (seen just north-northeast of Iceland). In terms of mountain ranges, the Alps, the Carpathians, the Pyrenees, and even part of the Caucasus mountains shows as clear blue spots in a field of white. Additionally, we see the mountains to the north, both in the northern Scandinavia and on Iceland. It is clear that mountainous areas have far more frost days than lower regions. 

Looking at the mean annual frost days in the top row of \autoref{fig:frost_days} it is, once again, apparent that the mid and late century \gls{126} and the mid century \gls{370} show relatively small changes in the cold climate. The primary changes is the number of frost days north and northeast of Iceland. This suggests that one of the first changes the climate will see is the slow eradication of the Arctic sea ice. Turning to the late century \gls{370}, in which the biggest change is seen, it seems that most of the northern frost days have disappeared and Iceland no longer sees sub-zero temperatures over the ocean. Additionally the land area of mainland Europe and Africa turns mostly white. In this case, only the higher, mountainous regions exhibit frost days while lower areas like Denmark, the Netherlands, and the British Isles will loose the last snowy days completely.

The residual plots in the second row of \autoref{fig:frost_days} tells much of the same story. The northern part of the domain is hit first and hardest, with a reduction of upwards of 200 annual frost days. The mainland also shows fewer frost days with the most apparent changes in the northern regions; Scandinavia and the Baltics. Interestingly enough, it does not seem like the mountain ranges have a bigger reduction in frost days than the rest of the mainland. 

The histograms in the third row of \autoref{fig:frost_days} visualises a similar, and expected, trend given the above spatial plots. The northern regions, which had upwards of 320 annual frost days are reduced all the way to 240. Similarly to the same plot for the summer days (\autoref{fig:summer_days}) the distributions of frost days seem to be directly scaled as the climate warms, this time just to the left. 

\subsection{Annual wet days}

The next index is the annual r10mm index. This index shows, different from the other two, the distribution of precipitation over the \gls{hclim} domain. It is defined as days in which the total precipitation exceeds 10 mm (and in this case is calculated as days in which the total daily precipitation exceeds $10 \frac{kg}{m^2}$). Different from the other indecies, this is not a frequently occurring event, and happens at max 120 days per year. Areas with a lot of precipitation are typically on the east coast, toward the Atlantic, where precipitation comes on form of rain, and in mountainous regions, where precipitation usually comes as snow.

\subsection{Anomaly Correlation Coefficient}

One last representation is needed to create a full picture of model performance on the climate task. This metric is the anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC), and it measures the temporal correlation for each pixel in the two models. This is used to make sure that the \gls{downscaling} model captures the temporal / seasonal changes in the variable. One thing is to model the spatial relationship for each variable, but a real climate emulator must be able to model the day to day changes as well. The ACC is computed as the temporal correlation between the \gls{ece} data and the \gls{hclim} data at each grid point.

    \centering

    \caption{Anomaly Correlation Coefficient (ACC) for each of the four target variables; TAS (Daily mean 2m temperature), TASMIN (Daily minimum 2m temperature), TASMAX (Daily maximum 2m temperature), and PR (Daily mean precipitation). The ACC is computed as the temporal correlation between the regridded \gls{ece} data and the \gls{hclim} data at each spatial point, for the entire 150 year time period. This plot only shows data from \gls{realisation} 1.}

In \autoref{fig:acc} the ACC can be seen for each variable of the \gls{hclim} data. The plots show where the two models fundamentally disagree on the temporal development of the data. As it might be expected, this is more apparent over land-masses as the spatial resolution is ever more important here, than over the ocean. Especially around the coastlines there is a lower correlation, due to the disagreement in where the coast actually is. For temperatures, the models seem to agree to a greater extend with slight exceptions, especially around the coast of the Mediterranean. For the precipitation however, the story is different. Here there are two remarkable features. One is that the grid is visible for this variable. This likely comes from the conservative interpolation used to create the regridded data set. The other features the to decrease in correlation on the eastern side of the map. It seems that the models have an agreement of the rain over the Atlantic ocean, but the further away from there the larger the disagreement. It is known, that precipitation is a difficult variable to model, as it is highly unreliable, and dependant on many features. It is therefore not unlikely that the \gls{ece} model fails to correctly capture the fine-resolution developments of the variable over the larger land-masses.

In \autoref{fig:acc} data is shown for both \gls{126} and \gls{370} however, there are not significant differences between the two. Similarly to what is seen in \autoref{fig:tas-11-timeseries} and \autoref{fig:pr-11-timeseries}, the difference between the \gls{gcm} and the \gls{ddm} for the two \glspl{scenario} is similar.
